The Complete Guide

The NMC Fitness to Practise process, explained by people who've been through it

Every stage from referral to outcome — what happens, how long it takes, and exactly what you can do to protect your registration.

If you have received a letter from the Nursing and Midwifery Council, take a breath. A referral is the start of a process, not a verdict — and most concerns never reach a hearing. This guide walks through every stage in plain English, written by healthcare professionals with lived experience of the process, not lawyers.

What is Fitness to Practise?

Fitness to Practise (FtP) is the process the NMC uses to make sure nurses, midwives and nursing associates on its register are safe to practise. When someone raises a concern — an employer, a patient, the police, or even a colleague — the NMC assesses whether the concern suggests your fitness to practise may be impaired, and whether any action is needed to protect the public. The same principles apply to HCPC and GMC registrants, with their own procedures.

The five stages of the NMC process

1

Screening

The NMC checks whether the concern is serious enough, and whether there is enough information, to open an investigation. Many concerns close here. You may be asked for information — engage calmly and take advice before responding.

2

Investigation

A case officer gathers evidence: statements, records, employer information. This commonly takes six months or more. You will usually be invited to respond — this is where a well-prepared reflective response starts to matter.

3

Case examiners

Two case examiners (one registrant, one lay) review everything and decide whether there is a case to answer. You normally have 28 days to submit your response first. They can close the case, issue advice or a warning, agree undertakings — or refer to a hearing.

4

Hearing

A Fitness to Practise Committee panel hears the evidence, decides the facts, then decides whether your fitness to practise is currently impaired. Hearings feel formal but move at a measured pace — knowing what to expect on the day removes much of the fear.

5

Outcome & any review

If impairment is found, the panel chooses the least restrictive outcome that protects the public: caution, conditions of practice, suspension, or — in the most serious cases — striking off. Suspensions and conditions are usually reviewed before they end, which is another opportunity to evidence your progress.

How long does it take?

The NMC works to an end-to-end target of around 15 months, and straightforward cases often conclude in six to nine months at the earlier stages. Cases that go all the way to a hearing regularly take longer — sometimes two years or more. The wait is hard, but it is also your greatest asset: every month is time to build the evidence of insight and remediation that shapes outcomes. Read more: How long does an NMC investigation take?

Can you keep working?

Usually, yes. A referral alone does not restrict your practice. Only an interim orders panel can impose restrictions — an interim conditions of practice order or, less commonly, an interim suspension — and only where it believes there may be a risk to the public while the case is investigated. Insight and remediation: the part you control

Across screening decisions, case examiner outcomes and hearing sanctions, one theme decides more cases than any other: has this professional genuinely understood what happened, and done something about it? That is insight and remediation, and unlike the allegations themselves, it is entirely within your control:

  • Targeted CPD that maps to the specific concern — with certificates for your evidence bundle.
  • Reflective writing that is honest, specific and anchored to the Code.
  • A reflective log maintained over months, not written in a panic the week before.
  • Changed practice you can point to, supported by supervision records and references.

Quick answers

How long does the process take?

Around 15 months end-to-end on average; six to nine months for many earlier-stage cases, longer if a hearing is needed.

Can I keep working?

Usually yes — unless an interim orders panel imposes restrictions while the case is open.

Do most referrals end in a hearing?

No. Many close at screening or with the case examiners — engagement, insight and remediation all help.

What are the possible sanctions?

Caution, conditions of practice, suspension, or striking off — always the least restrictive option that protects the public.
